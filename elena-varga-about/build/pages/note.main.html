Three values, not seven: the squint test

Elena Varga12 Oct 20264 min read

In 2016 a student asked me how I “actually see” a shadow. I answered with three values, filmed the answer, and it became the first lesson of Course 01.

Most beginners see seven, eight, a dozen greys. The eye is generous: it reports every tiny shift in the light, and the hand tries to copy all of them. The drawing goes flat and muddy, because no single tone is allowed to matter.

Half-close your eyes

Squinting throws away the detail and keeps the structure. Hold your pencil at arm’s length, half-close your eyes and look for a full minute before you mark anything. The small changes merge, and what is left is a few large shapes of light and dark.

Now count them. I count three: the paper, left white; one middle grey, for everything turned a little away from the light; and one dark, for the cast shadow and the deepest folds.

Squint. Count the values. I count three.

said in nearly every critique since 2016

Why three is enough

Three values are enough to describe form: a lit side, a turning side, a shadow. Every extra grey is a decision you have to defend, and most of them do not survive. When the big shapes are right, a drawing reads from across the room, before anyone sees a single line.

You can always add a fourth value at the very end, as an accent. You can rarely rescue a drawing that started with nine.

Try it tonight

  1. Put one object on a table under a single lamp: a cup, a shoe, a chair.
  2. Squint for a full minute. Find the paper, the grey and the dark.
  3. Block in the grey everywhere at once, in one even tone.
  4. Add the dark last, and only where it is darkest. Then stop.
